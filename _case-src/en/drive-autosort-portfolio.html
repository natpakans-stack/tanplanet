Empathize · Define

The real pain point wasn't the storage fee

The starting brief was “I don't want to pay for iCloud.” But one more question, what am I actually paying for, reshaped the whole problem.

I already had a big enough, fully paid-for hard drive plugged into my Mac, but it was just a place to dump files, not a library where I can find things. What iCloud really sells isn't gigabytes. It's things landing in the right place without me doing anything.

— The point of view that changed the whole brief

Once the problem was framed this way, the most sensible-looking fix, “just drag the files onto the HDD,” turned out to be worse. It swaps a monthly fee for manual effort paid every time a file comes in.

The real cost is the labor of sorting, not the space

A subscription is paid once per billing cycle. Sorting by hand costs attention every time something arrives, and that's a cost nobody keeps paying for long.

A folder nobody sorts becomes a folder nobody opens

Moving files to the HDD without anything to sort them leaves one pile of thousands of files you can't search. In the end you don't dare delete them and never open them, which is worse than leaving them on the phone.

A file extension tells you it's a photo, not what the photo is

My existing sorter could only send .jpg to Photos and .pdf to Documents. That's enough for documents but not for photos: with thousands of photos in one folder, I still couldn't find last month's slip.

Every existing solution asks you to upload private photos to the cloud

The services that are genuinely good at photo search all run on the provider's servers. Financial slips and photos of home have to leave the machine before you get the convenience back.

The deciding factor

The Vision framework already ships with macOS. It can classify images into 1,303 categories and read text in 30 languages, Thai included. It runs on the device, with no internet, no API key and no per-call cost. Everything needed was already on the machine; it only had to be wired up correctly.

Ideate · Prototype

To really replace iCloud, photos have to get from the phone to the hard drive on their own

However smart the sorter is, it means nothing if I have to plug the iPhone into the computer every time. The piece that makes the whole system a real cloud replacement is this path.

Plug in a cable and transfer every time

This is the old workflow that meant nobody actually did it. If you have to wait until you're home and find a cable, the photos just keep piling up on the phone.

Forward port 445 to the internet on the router

SMB is one of the most heavily scanned ports on the internet. Opening it directly for personal convenience is a bad trade, and it also needs dynamic DNS to keep up when the home IP changes.

Set up my own VPN into the house

The right result, but it means touching router settings, opening ports and managing keys and certificates myself. That's ongoing sysadmin work just to move photos.

The chosen route — Tailscale

A mesh VPN built on WireGuard that lets the iPhone and Mac talk directly over the tailnet's private IP, which stays the same no matter which network either device is on. No port forwarding, no dynamic DNS, not a single line of router config, and nothing exposed to the public internet.

Prototype

How it works — 5 steps

From a file that just landed in Inbox to its destination folder, without anyone pressing anything

01

Wake every 5 minutes

No real-time folder watching. This job doesn't need second-level speed, and waking on a schedule is cheaper than keeping a process running all the time.

02

Only pick up files untouched for over 1 minute

Skip recently modified files so a photo the phone is still uploading doesn't get moved half-finished into the wrong place.

03

First pass by extension

Photos · Videos · Documents · Design — unknown extensions aren't guessed; they stay in Inbox for a person to decide

04

Only photos go to Vision

Returns labels for what's in the photo, ordered by confidence · only if it looks like a screenshot does OCR run to read the text, since OCR is several times slower

05

Into a subfolder, or nowhere

Not confident = no guessing. It stays in Photos/ for a person to decide, the same rule as unknown extensions.

Test & Iterate

Three things I only learned by measuring against real files

Each one was an assumption that sounded reasonable on paper and turned out wrong when measured

I assumed Vision would recognize slips because its list has a label called receipt

Tested on 60 real slips, the receipt label never came back once. 59 of them got document and screenshot, all the same, because real slips aren't paper receipts photographed with a camera. They're screen captures from banking apps, which look exactly like screenshots.

Fix: if the image can't tell them apart, read the text on it instead. Vision supports Thai OCR, so it reads the image and looks for Thai words meaning reference code, PromptPay and amount paid. Because OCR is much slower than image classification, it only runs on images already classified as screenshots.

The word “fee” pulled stock purchase confirmations in as slips

When I built the list of words that mark a slip, I included the Thai word for fee because transfer slips often have it. Measured against 25 ordinary screenshots, 3 stock purchase confirmations were pulled in as slips because they also contain the word fee.

Fix: drop that word and keep only words specific enough not to show up in other kinds of financial documents. Measured again after the change, no ordinary screenshot was misfiled as a slip — lesson: a word that “usually appears” is not the same as a word that “only appears here.”

Among the 1,303 labels are words that look alike but mean different things

Vision's list includes hotdog prairie_dog eggplant cauliflower sunflower_seeds bobcat goldfish jellyfish. With substring matching, a hot dog photo becomes a dog photo, an eggplant becomes a plant, and an aquarium becomes dinner.

Fix: match whole lines only, never substrings. The fix took under a minute, but without reading through the entire label list first, there would have been no way to know it was needed.

The most valuable number in this table is zero

A sorter that occasionally gets things wrong is tolerable, since you'll find them when you open the folder. A tool that hides things in the wrong place is worse than no tool at all. So one rule held throughout the project: if it's not sure, it doesn't guess.

Define

Scope I deliberately left out

What was cut matters more to the tool's reliability than what was added

  • No guessing when unsure — a file staying where it is beats being moved somewhere its owner can't find
  • No external services at all — financial slips and private photos shouldn't be uploaded anywhere in exchange for tidy folders
  • No sorting logic in Swift — the classifier just prints raw output and the mapping to folder names lives in the script, so adding a category doesn't need a recompile
  • A missing HDD isn't treated as a failure — the job ends quietly and tries again next round, with no warning every 5 minutes
  • Already-sorted old files are left alone — it only handles new arrivals in Inbox; sorting the backlog is a call the owner should make

The best tool is the one you never have to open

Today it runs quietly every 5 minutes, with no screen, no notifications and nothing to press. Drop photos into Inbox, go do something else, and when you come back they're already in their folders.

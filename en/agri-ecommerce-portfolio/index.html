Farmers selling direct to buyers

The farmer platform is more than e-commerce — it's an ecosystem designed so everyone benefits, from farmers to consumers.

Mar 11, 20267 min read

SHARE

A platform that connects farmers directly with consumers — Buyer, Seller, Affiliate and Admin, every role shipped in 3 months

UX/UI DesignE-Commerce4 User RolesResponsive WebFigma

The problem

Why do Thai farmers need a platform of their own?

Thai farmers grow good produce but can only sell it through middlemen. Consumers want fresh food straight from the farm but don't know where to buy it, and the existing platforms weren't designed for farm products.

— Insight from research before the project started

Farmers can't reach online sales channels

Platforms like Shopee and Lazada are too complex for farmers: you need polished photos, have to set up shipping and promotions yourself, and the commission is high.

Consumers don't trust the quality

Buying fruit and vegetables online, you don't know where they came from, whether they're fresh or safe. There's no quality certification people can rely on.

Middlemen take a large margin

Vegetables that leave the farm at 20 baht reach the consumer at 80 baht. Farmers earn little, consumers pay a lot, and the system isn't fair.

The solution

Build an ecosystem where every party benefits

Direct-to-Consumer — cut out the middleman

Farmers list their own products, set their own prices and ship straight to consumers. They keep the full amount, and consumers pay less.

Regional products + OTOP

Products are grouped by region (North, Northeast, Central, East, West, South) plus OTOP goods, so consumers can easily find each area's local specialties.

Affiliate system — bring people in to help sell

Anyone can be an affiliate: share a product link and earn a commission. It builds a sales network without anyone holding stock.

Admin dashboard — full control of the system

Manage users, review products, view analytics and handle orders, so the team can run the platform efficiently.

Working around the constraints

3 months, 4 roles — nothing was easy

4 roles = 4x the complexity

Every feature had to be thought through from four points of view: what the buyer sees, how the seller manages it, how the affiliate tracks it, how the admin oversees it. A single screen could need four versions.

Solved with a shared component library — the same components, with content adjusted per role

3 months to ship the whole platform

The deadline was very tight. We had to prioritize, cut features that weren't essential, and focus on the core flow that makes the business actually work.

Used the MoSCoW method — clear Must / Should / Could / Won't buckets, with Musts done first

A wide range of users — from farmers to admins

Some farmers aren't comfortable with technology, so the UI had to be very simple, while admins needed a full set of power tools. Serving every group at once was hard.

Designed the seller flow as step-by-step, one tap at a time, while admins get a dashboard and data tables

Keeping pace with dev sprints

Design had to be at least one sprint ahead of development. Too late and the developers sit idle; too early and the work may need reworking later.

Clear design handoffs plus a spec sheet for every screen cut down the back-and-forth

Strategic Partners

Not built alone — the platform partnered with strong government agencies

What made the platform more than a "shopping app" — closer to "digital infrastructure for farmers" — was working with real government agencies, from the Ministry of Agriculture to Thailand Post, which handles logistics nationwide.

Why the partners mattered

Many farmers don't have a good smartphone and don't know how to start selling online. With the Department of Agricultural Extension handling onboarding and Thailand Post handling logistics, the platform actually reached farmers. We didn't just build an app and wait — we went out to meet farmers where they are.

Key Takeaways

What I learned from the farmer platform

Designing 4 roles at once is like chess — move one piece and every other piece is affected:

One buyer order → the seller needs a notification → the affiliate needs their commission → the admin needs to track all of it. Designing page by page without thinking about the cross-role flow causes a lot of problems later. Draw the system flow first, then design each page.

"You don't need to be good with technology, just follow along" — the sentence that changed the whole app's UX:

The real users are older farmers who may have used a smartphone for only a few years. The first seller flow had 8 steps, small buttons and technical wording. User testing with real farmers showed it had to shrink to 3 steps: register → photograph and price → get paid. That's enough.

Government partners are a shortcut to users digital marketing can't reach:

Many farmers aren't on Facebook and never see ads. But once the Department of Agricultural Extension ran on-site training for the platform, sign-ups poured in. The lesson: not every product needs an online growth hack — sometimes going to the users in person is more powerful.

The 3-month deadline forced choices — and that was the best thing that happened:

At first I wanted to design everything — wishlists, reviews, chat, coupons, gamification... but 3 months wasn't enough, so we had to choose. The result was a core flow that worked first (order → pay → ship), with the rest added later. "Do less, but ship it" beats "do a lot, but never finish."
